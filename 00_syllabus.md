## Course description

Dynamic process models are widely used in ecology and evolution and are a cornerstone of science. Such models, expressed as computer simulations or math, encode our understanding or intuition for how natural systems work and dynamically change over time. Process models are especially powerful when calibrated or confronted with data. This course includes process models and theory in ecology and evolution from DNA to ecosystems. However, the course is not a survey of models or theory. The goal is to empower you with fundamental modeling principles to understand existing models in your area of study and to make your own models for your unique research questions. We'll learn how to conceptualize a model, turn the concept into code, analyze model behavior, use the model to design field or lab studies, test hypotheses about how nature works, and make rigorous predictions and forecasts that incorporate uncertainty. I emphasize algorithmic thinking, with relatively simple math playing an important but supporting role rather than the traditional other way around. Algorithms are an intuitive way to focus on the step-by-step biology and observation processes. Assessment is based on coding assignments, reading recent literature, and an individual project.

## Key conceptual themes

* Dynamics at higher levels of organization emerge from lower-level processes
    * e.g. population cycles from interactions between individuals
    * e.g. adaptation of populations from selection on genes
* Models are algorithms for data-generating processes
* Data are generated by biological and observational processes
* Biological and observational processes are inherently algorithmic and computational
* Data generating processes can be modeled by algorithms at a variety of scales
* Mathematical models are often abstractions for biological or observational process algorithms
* Deterministic models often emerge as expectations or limits of stochastic individual-level processes
* Algorithms for data generating processes link biology to data
* Algorithms for training and inference enable us to evaluate evidence for different process-based ideas about how nature works and to make predictions that account for uncertainty

## Topics

Examples from ecology and evolution are used throughout

### Weeks 1-3

* Introduction
    * Data-generating processes
    * Models as algorithms rather than equations
    * Equations as useful abstractions to algorithms
    * Biological versus observation processes
    * Modeling workflows
* Agent- and individual-based simulation
    * Discrete events
    * Procedural programming
    * Object oriented programming and Python classes
    * Using high performance computing (HPC) resources
* Fundamental algorithms for biological processes
    * Stochastic state-changes: birth, death, movement, consumption, growth, infection, mutation, recombination, branching
    * Distributions: exponential, binomial, Poisson, multinomial, etc
        * derived from biological and physical mechanisms
        * pseudo-random numbers
        * variance-mean relationships
    * Gillespie algorithm for continuous-time processes
        * rates and exponential waiting times for discrete events

### Weeks 4-6

* Scaling up and emergence
    * From stochastic individuals to difference equations (discrete time)
    * From stochastic individuals to differential equations (continuous time)
        * algorithms to solve differential equations
    * Emergence of "deterministic skeleton plus stochasticity" algorithms
    * Emergence of complex dynamics from simple rules
* Randomness as an intrinsic biological mechanism
    * Consequences of stochasticity (demographic, environmental, dispersal, genetic)
        * drift, time to extinction, pattern formation, coexistence
    * Scale transition: interaction of nonlinearity and variation
* Algorithms for observation processes
    * Measurement stochasticity
        * Discrete-scale errors (e.g. counts)
        * Continuous-scale errors (e.g. weights)
        * Catastrophic errors (e.g. transcribing errors, shifted decimal points)
    * Distributions: normal, lognormal, t, Poisson-binomial, etc
* Training and inference algorithms for confronting models with data
    * Frequentist, likelihood, Bayesian
    * State-space algorithms
    * Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC)
    * Simulation based inference
    * Cross validation and predictive performance
    * Model comparison
    * Model checking algorithms

### Weeks 7-9

* Model analysis and simplification
    * Dimensional analysis and scaling
        * units, nondimensionalization, characteristic scales
    * Sensitivity analysis
        * which parameters matter?
        * local and global sensitivity, elasticity
        * perturbation algorithms
    * Dynamical systems analysis
        * equilibria, phase space, cycles, bifurcations, chaos
        * stability analysis
    * Linear approximation
    * Scale transition analysis
* Structured biological process models
    * Demographic models
        * life history algorithms
        * age and stage structure, individual states
        * model analysis
    * Space and movement
        * random walks, dispersal kernels, neighborhood interactions
        * from stochastic individuals to diffusion equations
    * Networks
    * Infectious processes
* Forecasting ecological processes
    * Propagating uncertainty
    * Predictive skill horizon and out-of-distribution challenges
    * Ensemble algorithms to improve predictive skill
        * bagging, boosting, stacking
* Miscellaneous topics
    * Empirical dynamic modeling
    * Machine learning meets process models (SINDy, neural differential equations)

### Weeks 10-14

  * Reading and discussion: applications in ecology and evolution
  * Individual project

## The ultimate learning goals

* You will have an understanding of the fundamental concepts and algorithms that underpin most process-based models
* You will be confident to derive process-based algorithms and models in your own research from these fundamental concepts and algorithms
* You will have a broad overview of how process-based models are currently being used in ecology and evolution

## Learning format

For the first two thirds, I'll lecture on fundamental concepts and you'll have reading and biweekly  practical coding assignments. Next, in a student-driven discussion and presentation format, we'll explore the recent literature in ecology and evolution to consider where process-based models are having an impact on research. In an individual project you'll construct process-based models for a system of your choice, generate simulated data and potentially use data you already have, which ideally will help you address your thesis research. Your final exam is a presentation of your individual project.

I hope that we can create an environment that is relaxed and nonjudgmental so that we'll all feel comfortable participating and also that all contributions are valued. I also hope that we can create an environment of respect for each other's learning processes and ideas. I'm envisioning a collaborative learning atmosphere. Collaboration is encouraged both in and out of class.

## Generative AI use policy

GenAI tools such as Claude Code and ChatGPT are useful productivity enhancers, especially for coding. They are potentially a game changer and democratizing force for modeling, which in my opinion is a good thing. As they are so new, I am still coming to terms with how best to incorporate them myself. I'm often amazed at their capability but also amused at their frequent mistakes. I'll be using these tools to help prepare class material, especially code. I'll share my experiences both positive and negative and encourage you to share experiences among our learning community. We'll consider emerging norms among the wider research community. However, GenAI tools are not a substitute for learning. If you don't learn the basics, you can't evaluate the output of GenAI tools and you'll inevitably be schooled by mistakes that you are ultimately responsible for. You own your code. In the research world, you will be called out by pre- or post-publication peer evaluation leading to embarrassment and loss of trust, and this would likely be career ending. In science, you cannot "fake it 'til you make it". Nobody cares about your graduate student grades, only your publications.

Devote this semester to your biological intelligence! To learn, you must use your brain. In the first part of the course, the goal is to learn fundamentals. AI tools that generate code are not allowed in assignments unless I explicitly direct you to use them. Use your own amazing brain and hand code for all the assignments. Understand your code line by line. You may use Large Language Models as a tutorial assistant to provide further feedback on your understanding of concepts and code, for example, by having them explain code to you, or having them explain mathematical concepts or techniques. For the individual project, you may use code-generating tools as they can allow you to increase the scope. By then, you will know what to ask for (i.e. you can intelligently prompt), what to expect in return, and we will have considered workflows that safeguard against mistakes. I will help you use your judgment here to balance project scope with your learning and research objectives.
